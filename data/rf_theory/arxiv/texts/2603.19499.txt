Geometric Performance Analysis of
Doppler-Based Positioning with a Single LEO
Satellite
Qi Liu, Marc Fernández-Temprado, Antoni Reus-Bergas, Gonzalo Seco-Granados, José A.
López-Salcedo
Abstract—Low Earth Orbit (LEO) satellites have
gained increasing attention as potential signal sources for
Positioning, Navigation and Timing (PNT) applications.
However, while most existing studies focus on multi-
satellite LEO constellations, the fundamental position-
ing performance achievable with a single LEO satellite
remains less extensively explored. This paper analyzes the
geometric characteristics and positioning performance of
single-satellite Doppler positioning through a theoret-
ical analysis of the Dilution of Precision (DOP) and
extensive numerical simulations. The results reveal a
strong directional error behavior, with severe error in
the cross-track direction but a significantly less error
along the satellite track, reflecting an intrinsic geometric
limitation of single-satellite LEO positioning. While these
features were already identified at the early stages of
satellite PNT missions, the present work provides an in-
depth analysis and unveils the fundamental limitations
and characteristics that could make LEO-based Doppler
positioning feasible nowadays, using one single satellite
only. In this way, the results of this work not only provide
valuable insights into the role of observational geometry
in Doppler navigation, but also offer guidance for opti-
mizing geometric configurations in future small or single-
satellite LEO constellations for strategic applications.

A. Role of Dilution of Precision (DOP)
As research on LEO-based PNT continues to
expand, it has become clear that beyond measure-
ment error mitigation and processing techniques, the
geometric configuration of the satellite constellation
plays a fundamental role in determining achievable
positioning accuracy [24]. This influence is commonly
characterized by the Dilution of Precision (DOP),
a metric that quantifies how satellite geometry
amplifies measurement errors into position domain
errors. LEO constellations exhibit rapidly changing
spatial geometry and highly dynamic visibility pat-
terns, leading to distinct DOP behaviors that vary
significantly over time and user location [25], [26].
Understanding these geometric effects is therefore
essential for interpreting LEO positioning perfor-
mance and for assessing how different constellation
designs, orbital parameters, and observation condi-
tions shape the overall error characteristics. Tyler
et al. [27] conducted a detailed comparison between
LEO constellations and GNSS based on aspects
such as link budget, number of visible satellites,
Geometric Dilution of Precision (GDOP), Horizontal
Dilution of Precision (HDOP), Vertical Dilution of
Precision (VDOP), and user ranging error. The
results show that the LEO constellations have a
better geometry than existing GNSS, while the study
did not fully analyze the relationship between DOP
and achievable positioning accuracy.
Intuitively, DOP reflects how well the satellites are
distributed across the sky and is inversely related
to the volume of the tetrahedron formed by the
user and the visible satellites. In addition, DOP
plays a key role in positioning accuracy because
the position error is determined by the product
of the measurement standard deviation and the
DOP. In other words, for the same measurement,
a larger DOP will lead to a larger position error,
whereas a smaller DOP limits this amplification
and yields better positioning accuracy. As shown
in Table I, a DOP value less than 1 typically
indicates an ideal satellite-receiver configuration [24].
Furthermore, through DOP optimization, a value of
approximately 0.9 can be achieved even with only
four visible GNSS satellites [28].

The conventional DOP formulation is derived
from pseudorange-based navigation solutions. In the
context of LEO satellite systems, Doppler measure-
ments offer a distinct advantage due to their strong
sensitivity to the rapid relative motion between the
satellites and the user. This makes Doppler-based
DOP (DDOP) analysis particularly relevant and of
growing interest for assessing the positioning poten-
tial of LEO observations. Psiaki [29] and Baron [30]
investigated a generalized DDOP analysis suitable
for LEO systems and derived an accuracy factor anal-
ogous to that used in GNSS, which can be employed
to evaluate precision. Ruben et al. [24] provided a
comprehensive comparison of code-based DOP and
DDOP analysis across existing LEO systems and
discussed the optimization steps to follow in building
novel LEO-PNT constellations for best positioning
performance. Although the analysis addressed sys-
tem performance, it did not explicitly evaluate how
key geometric variables, including orbital inclination
and user location, influence positioning accuracy via
DDOP.
B. Motivation for Single-Satellite Positioning
While large constellations of LEO satellites have
received a significant attention in recent years (e.g.
Starlink, Amazon LEO, etc.), small constellations
are also playing a relevant role in many ongoing
experimental and in-orbit demonstrations [31]. Many
countries are also considering the deployment of
institutional small LEO constellations to support
secure communications, which may eventually pro-
vide trusted PNT services as well. In parallel, within
the private sector, small satellite constellations are
increasingly attracting interest for applications such
as strategic surveillance, technology demonstration,
or as early proof-of-concept systems for future large-
scale constellations.
Moreover, in real-world operational conditions,
users may effectively experience the equivalent of
a small LEO constellation due to environmental
constraints, such as in indoor environments, urban

--- PAGE BREAK ---

canyons, or other signal-degraded or denied scenar-
ios. In all these situations, positioning based on a
very limited number of satellites or, in the extreme
case, relying on a single LEO satellite, becomes a
relevant capability whose fundamental performance
limits are not yet fully understood.
These factors prompted our focus on studying the
positioning performance of single-satellite LEO cases
in this paper, yielding the following contributions.
First, we establish a theoretical framework that ana-
lytically relates DDOP to single-satellite positioning
error, explicitly decoupling the error propagation
into along-track and cross-track directions. This
model is validated through an analysis of different
satellite passes to ensure its reliability under varying
geometric conditions. Secondly, based on this vali-
dated framework, we conduct a systematic sensitivity
analysis to quantify how single-satellite positioning
performance is modulated by four critical factors: the
observation sampling rate, the start time of the pass,
orbital inclination, and the user’s position. Collec-
tively, these investigations reveal the error evolution
patterns driven by dynamic LEO geometries, serving
as a theoretical basis for mitigating geometric singu-
larities and finding the best conditions for user’s to
implement single-satellite positioning. These findings
provide a reference framework for optimizing user-
side positioning performance in LEO-PNT scenarios
under existing or future satellite constellations.
II. Methodology
This section introduces the methodology used to
assess the performance of single-satellite Doppler-
based positioning. First, the signal model for Doppler
measurements will be presented, including the single-
epoch and the multiple-epoch cases. Then, the geo-
metrical problem of solving the user’s position will
be formulated by means of Nonlinear Least Squares
(NLS) estimation. Finally, the NLS framework will
set the grounds for computing the DDOP for the
problem at hand.

[29]. In the specific case of DDOP, two key aspects

III. Numerical Results
A. Simulation Setup
Firstly, we describe the common simulation setup
in our experiments. The receiver is assumed to be
located at [41.3976◦, 2.1497◦, 60m], corresponding to
Barcelona, Spain. The simulated dataset is generated
at 1 s resolution over 350 s. Satellite trajectories
are generated from TLE data of the Orbcomm 108,
and only single satellite pass geometry is considered.
Unless otherwise stated, all experiments are con-
ducted using the simulation setup described above.
To more clearly illustrate the relationship between
positioning errors and the satellite’s orbital geom-
etry, all positioning errors reported are expressed
in the Radial-Transverse-Normal (RTN) coordinate
frame. The frame is constructed with respect to the
satellite that is closest to the user, following the
procedure outlined in Fig. 1. In this coordinate frame,
the T-axis points along the satellite’s direction of
motion (i.e., the along-track direction), while the N-
axis corresponds to the cross-track direction.
To assess the goodness of the theoretical DDOP
obtained in (22)-(25), the values obtained will be
compared to the actual positioning errors incurred
when solving the user’s position using the iterative
WLS framework described in Section II.C. To do so,
positioning errors over 1000 Montecarlo simulations

--- PAGE BREAK ---

will be computed, using two different satellite orbits
in order to have some diversity on the impact of the
actual satellite pass. Later, average results for all
possible passes will be obtained.
Doppler measurements are obtained by computing
the noise-free Doppler shift based on the satellite
and receiver state according to (2), and subsequently
corrupting them with zero-mean Gaussian noise to
obtain (4). The receiver state estimation is performed
using a WLS algorithm, employing all observations
within the observation period. To study the accuracy
of the theory, the initial position is set to the true
value and each trial uses the same satellite geome-
try and receiver initial position, while independent
realizations of measurement noise.
B. Consistency Validation of DDOP Analysis and
Positioning Errors
The comparison between the actual single-satellite
positioning errors and the theoretical bounding pro-
vided by the DDOP is shown in Fig. 2 for two
different satellite passes. In each of the upper and
lower maps of this figure, the ground projection of
the passes is indicated by a light blue solid line. In
turn, the blue dots represent positioning error of each
individual simulation with respect to the reference
position. To describe the variation patterns of errors
in different directions, the horizontal error is decom-
posed into along-track and cross-track components
as defined in Fig. 1.
The empirical 95% confidence ellipse (solid green)
computed from the positioning errors obtained from
the simulations, is compared with the corresponding
theoretical 95% ellipse (red dashed) predicted by
the DDOP computation. The 95% confidence ellipse
is derived from the covariance matrix, and the
eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the covariance matrix
determine the axis lengths and orientation of the el-
lipse, respectively. The axes are scaled by the square
root of the chi-square value corresponding to the 95%
probability level with two degrees of freedom, i.e.,
2,0.95 = 5.991. For the first satellite pass shown in
Fig. 2(a), the simulated along-track and cross-track
errors are 28 m and 9932 m, respectively, while their
corresponding theoretical values are 27 m and 9629
m, respectively. For the second satellite pass shown in
Fig. 2(b), which is nearly orthogonal to the first pass,
the simulated along-track and cross-track errors are
20 m and 5561 m, respectively, while their theoretical
counterparts are 19 m and 5384 m, respectively. Even
though the magnitude of the positioning error varies
across different satellite trajectories, the estimated
empirical 95% confidence ellipse and the theoretical
95% ellipse turn out to be in high agreement. This

E. Impact of the Observations Start Time
In the previous analysis, the impact of the ob-
servation time interval was evaluated by fixing the
observation start time at the satellite rise epoch. In
practical scenarios, however, the temporal placement
of the observation window may be chosen by the user
and this could have an impact on the underlying
measurement geometry, thereby affecting positioning
accuracy. In order to shed light onto this issue, we
further investigate the positioning performance un-
der different observation start times while comparing
multiple observation sampling times.
Fig. 5 represents cross-track positioning errors
obtained using four equally spaced Doppler ob-
servations from a single LEO satellite under dif-
ferent observation sampling time. The horizontal
axis denotes the time offset between the center of
the observation window and the epoch of closest
satellite–user approach, where zero corresponds to
the minimum satellite–user range. Finally, the ver-
tical axis denotes the cross-track error. It can be
seen that for all sampling time configurations, the
cross-track error exhibits a clear U-shaped behavior
with respect to the time offset, reaching its minimum
when the observation window is centered near the
closest approach. For a fixed observation sampling
time, shifting the center of the observation window
away from the point of closest approach leads to a
degradation in cross-track accuracy. This behavior
reflects the increasing asymmetry of the satellite
pass geometry, which in turn weakens the geometric
strength of the positioning solution. In addition,
increasing the observation sampling significantly re-
duces the cross-track error for the same time offset.
Even with a limited number of measurements, the
cross-track error can be reduced from 105m to 103m
just by increasing the observation sampling time
from 10 to 80 s.
Fig. 6 shows along-track positioning errors ob-
tained using four equally spaced Doppler observa-
tions from a single LEO satellite under different
observation sampling times. In contrast to the cross-
track component, the along-track error exhibits a
generally smaller magnitude and a weaker sensitivity
to the observation timing offset. Although the along-
track error decreases as the center of the observation
window shifts, this change is less pronounced than
in the cross-track direction. Furthermore, extending
the observation sampling time can improve the along-
track error from approximately 600m to 100m. And
the along-track error demonstrates smoother trends
and smaller dynamic ranges compared to the cross-
track error, suggesting that along-track direction is
inherently more robust under limited-measurement
conditions in single-satellite, multi-epoch LEO posi-
tioning.
The above results verified that both the obser-
vation sampling time and the temporal placement
of the observation window play a critical role in
single-satellite LEO Doppler positioning. Positioning
the observation window’s center point closer to the
closest approach and employing a longer sampling
time significantly improves algorithm performance
when few Doppler measurements are available. It can
also be readily observed from Fig. 5 and Fig. 6, when
the observation sampling is set to 60 s, both cross-
track and along-track errors can reach a relatively
stable state, provided that the center of the selected

F. Impact of Orbital Inclination
The orbital inclination determines the ground
track of a satellite and therefore directly influ-
ences the geometric evolution of the Line-of-Sight
(LOS) vector between the satellite and the receiver.
Intuitively, changing the orbital inclination alters
the direction and shape of the satellite’s ground
trajectory relative to the receiver, which may lead
to different LOS variation patterns during a satellite
pass. These geometric differences can significantly
affect the performance for single-satellite positioning.
It is important to note that the term inclination
Fig. 7: Skyplot with different orbital inclinations
(arrows indicate the direction of motion).
refers herein to the relative orientation between
the satellite orbital plane and the receiver’s local
reference plane, rather than the classical orbital
inclination defined in TLE files.
To investigate this effect, we analyze the position-
ing performance under different inclination configu-
rations, as illustrated in Fig. 7. In the experiment,
the inclination of the satellite’s orbital plane is varied
while keeping the observation duration and measure-
ment conditions unchanged. This controlled setup
allows the resulting positioning errors to reveal how
different orbital inclinations influence the positioning
performance of a single LEO satellite.
Fig. 8 shows the cross-track positioning error as a
function of the orbital plane inclination. The effect
of orbital inclination is represented here by the
satellite’s maximum elevation angle rather than by
the inclination itself, because the former is more
directly related to the satellite–user geometry during
a pass and provides a clearer way to present its vari-
ation. During a satellite pass, the elevation changes
approximately symmetrically, increasing from low
elevation to a peak and then decreasing again, which
makes the associated variation in estimation perfor-
mance easier to visualize. Therefore, the maximum
elevation angle is used as the horizontal axis in
the figure. A pronounced peak is observed at an
maximum elevation angle of 90◦, corresponding to to
the satellite trajectory passing directly overhead the
user’s receiver. In this configuration, the LOS vectors
remain nearly coplanar throughout the satellite pass,
leading to limited geometric diversity in the cross-
track direction. As a result, cross-track observability
is significantly degraded, with the estimation error
nearly reaching 105m.
In contrast, when the orbital inclination decreases
below 90◦, the cross-track error initially decreases
due to the enhanced geometric diversity introduced
by the lateral offset of the satellite trajectory. How-
ever, when the inclination continues to move further
away, the error gradually increases again, as the
degradation in overall measurement geometry and
the associated increase in DDOP outweigh the bene-
fits brought by additional cross-track information.
This results in a non-monotonic, symmetric error
profile.
Fig. 9 illustrates the variation pattern of the
along-track error, which exhibits an opposite trend
compared to the cross-track error. The minimum
error is minimized to within approximately 102 m
when the maximum satellite elevation approaches
90◦. As the elevation deviates from this configu-
ration, along-track observability diminishes, causing
the estimation error to increase continuously.

IV. Conclusion
In conclusion, we investigated the positioning per-
formance of the single-satellite LEO case based on
Doppler measurement, with a particular focus on the
geometric characteristics and error behavior induced
by limited observation geometry. A comprehensive
numerical analysis was conducted to examine the
consistency between DDOP-based theoretical predic-
tions and positioning errors, as well as the influence
of key geometric factors, including observation num-
bers, sampling times, start time, orbital inclination
and user positions.
The results demonstrate that single-satellite LEO
positioning exhibits pronounced directional charac-
teristics in its error distribution. In particular, the
cross-track direction is severely weakly constrained,
leading to rapid error growth and extreme degrada-
tion under certain geometric configurations, with the
maximum error reaching 108 m. Moreover, the results
show that the largest positioning error occurs along
the satellite-motion direction, indicating that this
behavior reflects an intrinsic geometric limitation.
In contrast, the along-track direction is inherently
better constrained in single-satellite Doppler posi-
tioning, as the Doppler observables provide stronger
sensitivity through the projection of the satellite ve-
locity onto this direction. As a result, the positioning
error is minimized along the satellite trajectory and
remains comparatively stable across different ground
tracks and orbital configurations.
Moreover, based on the numerical results presented
in this paper, several practical design guidelines can
be concluded for single-satellite LEO-based position-
ing:
1) Increasing the sampling interval significantly
improves positioning performance up to ap-
proximately 60 s, after which the improve-
ment becomes limited. Therefore, in practical
single-satellite Doppler positioning applications,
a sampling interval on the order of tens of
seconds (around 60 s) provides a good balance
between geometric diversity and measurement
availability.
2) Positioning accuracy strongly depends on the
temporal placement of the observation window
within the satellite pass, the observation interval
should therefore be selected to cover the central
portion of the satellite pass, with the best perfor-
mance obtained when the observation window is
centered near the closest satellite–user approach.
3) The satellite orbital geometry also has a sig-
nificant impact on positioning performance,
the cross-track error becomes largest when the
satellite passes directly overhead due to the
nearly coplanar observation geometry. Consid-
ering both along-track and cross-track perfor-
mance, satellite passes with maximum elevation
around 60° provide the best overall positioning
performance and are therefore preferable for
single-satellite Doppler positioning.
These findings highlight the fundamental limi-
tations and characteristics of single-satellite LEO
positioning and provide useful guidance for im-
proving observation geometry to achieve enhanced

presented analysis establishes a theoretical and nu-

this framework to real measurement validation.
